Lesson 03 · The Commodity That Got an Asterisk

Memory: DRAM, NAND, HBM & Why Micron Is Suddenly Wanted

Last lesson I rated memory the thinnest moat on the whole chain — a price-driven commodity. So why are investors crowding into Micron right now? Because one corner of memory just stopped behaving like a commodity. That exception, and how to tell it from a mirage, is today's single win.

The honest tension

In Lesson 01 the very last bar on the staircase was "Commodity memory — cyclical, ●○○○○", with MU (Micron) as the example. The logic still holds: memory is mostly sold on price. Yet in 2026 Micron's stock and order book are red-hot, with its entire year of premium memory sold out in advance.1

That isn't a contradiction — it's the most useful nuance in memory investing. To see it, you first need to know that "memory" is really three different products.

1 · DRAM — the working desk

Commodity

Moat ●●●●● · MU · Samsung · SK Hynix

Dynamic RAM is the fast, short-term volatile memory a computer actively works in — the open desk where today's data sits while the processor uses it. Fast but forgetful: power off, it's blank. A stick of DDR5 from any of the three makers is interchangeable, so it's bought on price. This is the commodity from Lesson 01.

2 · NAND — the filing cabinet

Commodity

Moat ●●●●● · MU · Samsung · SK Hynix · Kioxia

NAND flash is non-volatile storage — the filing cabinet that remembers when the power's off. It's what's inside SSDs and phones. Slower than DRAM, far cheaper per gigabyte, and even more of a price-war commodity — it has a fourth and fifth maker, so margins are usually the thinnest in memory.

3 · HBM — DRAM, stacked for AI

Re-rating

Moat ●●●●● · SK Hynix · MU · Samsung

High-Bandwidth Memory isn't a new chemistry — it's ordinary DRAM dies stacked vertically (8–12 high) and wired to the processor through thousands of microscopic vertical connections. It sits right next to an AI GPU and feeds it data at colossal speed. It is hard to manufacture, designed-in alongside the customer's chip, and currently sold out. That's why its moat dots are different from its own cousins above.

Why HBM breaks the commodity rule

An AI GPU is a glutton: it can crunch numbers far faster than ordinary memory can deliver them. The processor ends up starving, waiting on data — engineers call this the memory wall. HBM smashes through it by trading a long, thin pipe for a short, enormously wide one:

Three things flow from that picture, and together they un-commoditise HBM:

Hard to make

Yields are low, packaging is advanced

Stacking a dozen wafer-thin dies and threading thousands of vertical connections through them wrecks yields. Most makers can't do it well — so supply is scarce by nature, not by cartel.

↓
Designed-in

Qualified alongside the customer's GPU

HBM is co-engineered and tested with the specific accelerator (e.g. NVIDIA's). Once you're in a design, you're not swapped out mid-generation on price — the opposite of interchangeable.

↓
Three makers only

A genuine oligopoly with a waitlist

Only SK Hynix, Micron and Samsung make HBM at all — and 2026 output is pre-sold. Scarce supply + concentrated skill = real pricing power, for now.

So why Micron, right now?

Micron (MU) is one of only three companies on Earth that can make HBM — and it's gaining, having overtaken Samsung to roughly 21% of the HBM market behind SK Hynix's ~62%.2 But the demand surge has two engines, and the second is the clever part:

The 2026 squeeze, in four numbers

Sold outMicron's entire 2026 HBM output is committed under long-term contracts before the year is out.1
~93%Share of the big three's combined capacity now tilted toward HBM for AI — starving ordinary DRAM supply.1
+90%Jump in ordinary DRAM prices in Q1 2026 vs. the prior quarter, as that starved supply collides with demand.3
$60–100Price of one HBM3E module — vs. roughly $5–10 for the same capacity of plain DDR5.1

Engine one: HBM itself sells at a huge premium and is sold out. Engine two: making all that HBM consumes the factory space that used to make ordinary DRAM — so even the boring commodity is now scarce and spiking. Micron wins on the premium product and on the shortage it helped create.

Now re-rate the moat — carefully

Here's the payoff, drawn the same way as Lesson 01's staircase. The same product family, split in two:

◆ Moat Lens

HBM's moat is real but conditional. It rests on scarce supply and being designed-in — not on a structural lock like ASML's EUV or TSMC's process. Three forces can erode it: (1) the makers are spending enormously to add capacity (Micron alone plans ~$200B1) — and new capacity is how every memory boom has historically ended in glut; (2) the buyer is concentrated — sell mostly to NVIDIA and NVIDIA holds the whip; (3) it's still memory, so the cycle is delayed, not repealed. Your job from Lesson 01's mission: don't mistake a cyclical peak — even a spectacular one — for a permanent moat. HBM is exactly the test case that skill exists for.

Check yourself

Pull these from memory — no scrolling back. The effort is what makes it stick.

Q1

What is HBM, most precisely?

HBM is the same DRAM silicon, stacked vertically and bonded to the processor with a very wide bus — not a new material, and not storage (that's NAND). Because it's hard to make and designed-in, it escapes the pure-commodity trap that plain DRAM sits in.

Q2

Why are ordinary DRAM prices spiking in 2026, not just HBM?

Roughly 93% of the big three's capacity tilted to HBM for AI, which consumes the fab space that used to make commodity DRAM. Less ordinary supply meets steady demand → prices jumped ~90% in a quarter. Micron benefits from both the premium product and the shortage.

Q3

What most threatens HBM's current moat over the next cycle?

HBM's moat is scarcity-based, and every memory boom in history has ended when makers over-invested into a glut — Micron's own ~$200B expansion is the seed of that. The other options aren't the live risk: the chokepoint that protects HBM is its own tight, hard-to-build capacity, not lithography or EDA.

The one idea to keep

If you remember nothing else: memory is three products — DRAM (working desk), NAND (filing cabinet), HBM (DRAM stacked for AI) — and only HBM has temporarily escaped commodity status, on scarcity and design-in, not on a permanent lock. Micron is wanted because it sells the scarce premium product and rides the shortage that making it creates. The investor's discipline is to ask whether that's a re-rating or a peak. Next lesson we'll build the tool for exactly that judgement: reading the semiconductor cycle, so you never buy the top thinking it's a moat.
