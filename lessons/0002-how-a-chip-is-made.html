Lesson 02 · The Process

How a Chip Is Made

A finished chip starts as sand and ends as the most precise object humanity mass-produces. Walk the journey once, with pictures, and the abstract "value chain" from Lesson 1 becomes physical — and you'll see exactly where the chokepoints sit.

First, the whole supply chain on one page

The headline pipeline has four stages. Underneath it sits a layer of enabling suppliers — the companies that sell tools, machines and materials into the pipeline. That bottom layer is where the quietest, widest moats live.

◆ Moat Lens

Notice the asymmetry: thousands of companies can design chips (top-left), but only a handful can supply EUV machines or leading-edge fabrication. Money flows up the diagram during every boom — which is why "sell shovels in a gold rush" so often points at the bottom row.

From sand to a finished chip

Stage 2 — fabrication — is the magic. Zoom into it. The raw input is literally purified sand (silicon); the output is a wafer covered in hundreds of identical chips.

Here's the full sequence in words — the front-end (building the chip on the wafer) then the back-end (turning the wafer into shippable chips):

Grow & slice the wafer

Purified silicon is grown into a single giant crystal (an ingot), then sliced into mirror-smooth discs.

Materials suppliers (mostly Japanese)

Build the chip, layer by layer

The wafer cycles through deposition → lithography → etch → doping dozens of times, stacking up transistors and wiring. This loop is the heart of the fab — diagram below.

Foundry, using WFE machines

Test on the wafer

Probes electrically test every die while still on the wafer; the duds are marked. The fraction that work is the yield — a make-or-break number.

Foundry + test-equipment makers

Dice, package & final test

The wafer is cut into individual dies, each sealed into a protective package with electrical connections, then tested again before shipping.

OSAT (back-end)

📖 Your book《芯片战争》opens Chapter 5 with this exact sequence — sand → ingot → wafer → pattern → package — and notes that of the several hundred process steps, lithography is the most important. That's our next focus.

The heart of the fab: the photolithography loop

Step 2 above isn't done once — it repeats for every layer of the chip, often 50–100 times. Each pass prints one layer of pattern and carves it into the wafer. This repeating loop is why fabs are so hard, so slow, and so capital-hungry.

Tap each step in the loop to see what physically happens — and which company owns it.

Tap a numbered step above. Pay attention to step 3 — it's the one only one company on Earth can do.

1 · Deposit film

Equipment: deposition tools · AMAT · LRCX

A microscopically thin film (metal, insulator, or silicon) is laid across the whole wafer — the raw material of this layer. Done by deposition machines from Applied Materials and Lam Research.

2 · Coat with photoresist

Material: photoresist (mostly Japanese suppliers)

The wafer is spun and coated with a light-sensitive chemical called photoresist. Where light hits it next, its chemistry will change.

3 · Expose — the lithography step

The chokepoint

Equipment: lithography scanners · ASML (EUV: sole supplier)

A lithography machine projects the circuit pattern onto the resist using light. The smallest features need EUV light, and only ASML on the entire planet can build an EUV machine. Every leading-edge chip passes through this one box, made by one company. That is what a chokepoint looks like physically.

4 · Develop

Part of the litho cell · TEL (Tokyo Electron)

The exposed resist is washed; the light-struck parts dissolve away, leaving the pattern standing on the wafer — like developing a photograph.

5 · Etch

Equipment: etch tools · LRCX · AMAT

Chemicals or plasma carve away the film everywhere the resist doesn't protect — transferring the pattern permanently into the layer. Lam Research dominates etch.

6 · Strip & repeat

Then back to step 1 — 50–100 times

The leftover resist is cleaned off. The wafer is now one layer taller — and the whole loop begins again for the next layer. Repeat 50–100 times to build the full chip.

What the finished chip actually is

The result of all those loops is a microscopic skyscraper: transistors on the ground floor, then a dense stack of copper wiring above them connecting everything.

Why this gets harder every generation — the moat, made of money

Each time the industry shrinks to a smaller node, the loop above gets harder and the bill gets bigger. The result is the most important number in the whole industry: how many companies can still afford to play. Drag the slider and watch it collapse.

This is the engine of the foundry moat. At 130 nm, ~30 companies could compete — almost no protection. At the leading edge, the survivors collapse to two or three, because a single chip now costs hundreds of millions just to design (and billions to build a fab for). 📖 Your book《芯片战争》(Ch. 18) pins the turning point at 28 nm: the first time in Moore's Law history that a smaller node made chips more expensive, not less — which is why so many firms quit.

◆ Moat Lens

Every diagram points the same way. The hardest, least-copyable steps — the Expose step (ASML), process control that keeps yield high (KLA), and running the whole loop at the leading edge (TSMC) — are exactly the links with the widest moats. And the slider shows why: brutal capital intensity keeps the rival count near zero. When you analyse a chip stock, ask: which step of this picture does the company own, how many rivals can do it, and is that number shrinking or growing? Few & shrinking → widening moat. Many & growing → commodity.

Check yourself

From memory — the recall is what builds retention.

Q1

Why does building a single chip take the lithography loop dozens of times instead of just once?

A chip is a 3D stack of 50–100 layers (transistors plus a tower of wiring). The deposit → expose → etch loop builds exactly one layer per trip, so it repeats once per layer. See the cross-section above.

Q2

In the supply-chain map, which row tends to hold the widest, least-contested moats?

The bottom row — EDA, equipment (esp. lithography), and process control. Thousands of firms can design chips; only a handful can supply the machines. Money flows up into that row in every boom.

Q3

"Yield," the number flagged at the wafer-test step, refers to —

Yield = the fraction of dies that pass testing. At the leading edge it can make or break a node's economics — and high yield is a big part of why TSMC's manufacturing is so hard to match. We'll dig into this in the TSMC lesson.

Q4

As the process node shrinks toward the leading edge, the number of companies able to manufacture there —

It collapses — ~30 at 130nm, ~10 at 28nm, just 4 at 7nm (《芯片战争》ch.18). Cost explodes while survivors vanish, and that scarcity is exactly what makes the leading-edge foundry moat so wide.

The one idea to keep

A chip is built by repeating a deposit → expose → etch loop 50–100 times to stack transistors and wiring, and the single hardest step in that loop — Expose, i.e. lithography — is controlled by one company. The physical process and the moat map are the same map — and every node shrink makes that map harder to copy.
